What is Persistent Homology?

Persistent homology is the tool I reach for when I want to ask data a question that statistics cannot phrase: what shape are you? This post is the ten-minute version, with toys. Everything below is computed live in your browser — click things. If you want the full guided tour (the Vietoris–Rips complex, the ℤ/2 linear algebra, stepping through the actual matrix reduction), I made a separate full-screen version: The Shape of a Point Cloud.

The question statistics can’t ask

Here are twenty-four measurements — two features of some cells, two sensor readings, whatever you like. Your eye needs a tenth of a second to notice the important thing about them: these points sit on a loop.

The average lands in the one place where there is no data. The trend line shrugs. Means, variances, and regressions summarize where the data is; none of them can say the sentence “there is a hole in the middle.” That sentence is a statement about shape, and the branch of math that makes it precise is topology. Persistent homology is topology turned into something you can run on a matrix.

Why expect data to have a shape at all? Because measurements are usually many, but the dials behind them are few — a face photographed under a turning light is a million pixels, yet only one angle truly varies. This is the manifold hypothesis: high-dimensional data tends to lie near a low-dimensional shape, because the process that generated it had only a few degrees of freedom. Anything periodic traces a loop. Shapes are what generating processes look like from the outside.

Give the dust a body

A finite set of points is topologically just dust. The classic trick: grow a disk of radius r around every point and study the union of disks instead. Drag the slider.

Small r: dust. Large r: one featureless blob. Somewhere in between, the union of disks briefly becomes the shape your eye saw. So which radius is the right one? Here is the idea that makes the whole subject work: refuse to choose. Sweep r from zero to huge and keep a diary of every topological event along the way — when each feature appears (its birth) and when it disappears (its death). That diary is called a barcode.

Reading a barcode

The simplest features are connected components, counted by H0. At r = 0 every point is its own island; as disks grow, islands merge, and each merge ends one island’s story. Draw each lifespan as a horizontal bar:

Hover a bar to light up the island whose life it records. The short bars die young — points that quickly joined a neighbor; that is the texture of noise. The two long bars are the two genuine clusters. And one bar never dies: at the end everything is a single blob, and that component lives forever.

The more interesting characters are the holes themselves, counted by H1. A loop is born at the radius where a ring of disks first closes, and dies at the radius where its middle fills in:

Switch to the figure eight: two holes, two long bars. Switch to random scatter: little loops flicker into existence by accident and are filled almost immediately. This is the entire reading lesson: long bars are features of the data; short bars are accidents of the sampling. And it comes with a warranty — the stability theorem says that perturbing the points a little moves no bar’s endpoints by more than a little, which is why you may trust a barcode computed from noisy data.

Under the hood, none of this needs the plane. The algorithm only ever reads pairwise distances, builds a growing family of simplicial complexes from them, and reduces one boundary matrix over ℤ/2 — the whole subject is a null space and a quotient, which is exactly why I can point it at data in ℝ1000. If you want to see the matrix reduction happen column by column (it is genuinely satisfying), that is §7–8 of the full tour.

Why I care

In my own work the “point cloud” is usually the latent space of a language model — token or sentence embeddings, attention patterns, activations. Those spaces are high-dimensional, noisy, and have no ground-truth geometry, but they plausibly do have shape: the processes that generate language have structure, and the manifold hypothesis says that structure should leave fingerprints. Topology is attractive here precisely because it needs no alignment between spaces — you can compare the shape of two embedding spaces without ever putting them in the same coordinates.

Resources

Tools I use for persistent homology on model internals. I maintain forks for most of these, because they are often not built for LLM analysis — the data is noisy and does not have any ground-truth representation the way biological data does.

  • eff-ph — effective PH pipelines; evaluated on toy examples and biological data with ground truth.
  • flooder — fast GPU-friendly filtration construction for large point clouds.
  • HOLE — visualizing the topology of neural nets in H0; originally evaluated on image-classification networks.
  • PH on attention maps — persistent homology applied to transformer attention maps.

TDA for NLP is a niche but quickly growing area. Start with Adaku Uchendu and Thai Le’s survey, Unveiling Topological Structures from Language: A Comprehensive Survey of Topological Data Analysis Applications in NLP, and her companion list, AwesomeTDA4NLP.

And for the mathematics itself: Robert Ghrist’s essay Barcodes: the persistent topology of data is the classic next step; Edelsbrunner & Harer’s Computational Topology is the standard textbook; Ripser is what the professionals use.
