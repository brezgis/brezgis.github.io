Language Organizes Color Geometry in Multilingual Encoders

Anna Brežģis

Symmetry and Geometry in Neural Representations (NeurReps) 2026

OpenReview ↗

Across six languages and five multilingual encoders, each of Berlin and Kay’s (1969) eleven basic color terms is represented by the mean embedding of its corpus contexts, giving one representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) for each of the 30 language–encoder pairs. RDMs of the same language correlate at a mean Spearman ρ = .495 across encoders, compared with ρ = .231 for different languages within the same encoder.

Method

For each of the eleven basic color terms (black, blue, brown, gray, green, orange, pink, purple, red, white, yellow) in English, Russian, Spanish, Italian, Greek and Turkish, about 1,200 key-word-in-context lines are mined from CC-100. Five multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM-R base and large, mDeBERTa-v3 and mmBERT) each read every line, and one hidden layer at about two-thirds depth is kept. Each occurrence of a word is represented by the mean over its subword tokens, and a word’s centroid is the mean of its occurrence vectors. Where a language has two words for blue, as Russian does with синий and голубой, the BLUE representation is the mean of the two. For each language–encoder cell, the pairwise cosine distances among the eleven centroids give an 11 × 11 RDM, and pairs of RDMs are compared by Spearman correlation.

Results

RDMs from the same language computed by different encoders agree far more strongly than RDMs from the same encoder across languages (mean Spearman ρ = .495 versus .231). Language identity is also recoverable from a held-out RDM. Classifying each cell by its nearest language prototype succeeds in 27 of 30 cells, against 11 of 30 for encoder identity.

The language signature is not driven by any single term, and it replicates on five disjoint samples of sentences per word.

Discussion

Across five encoders and six languages, the relational geometry of basic color terms behaves as a stable property of the language as used. It is nearly invariant to the sentences that instantiate it and largely invariant to the encoder that computes it, yet distinctive enough to identify its language from held-out data.

These findings are exploratory. The encoders’ pretraining data overlap, so shared text may contribute to same-language stability, and nothing here shows the effect is specific to color. Testing other lexical domains, and comparing against human color-similarity judgments, is a natural next step.
