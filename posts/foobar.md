Every computer science student encounters *foobar* sooner or later in their education. Usually without any preamble or explanation of convention, *foobar* or *foo* is written on blackboards, used in examples, and appears in textbooks. Probably as something like this, from K-State's [*Introduction to Python*](https://textbooks.cs.ksu.edu/intro-python/06-functions/index.print.html):

And the thing is, like [so many other things in higher education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_curriculum), nobody ever explains what foobar means. Rarer still, nobody thinks to ask. Students just seem to acquire it implicitly, or just accept it as a fact of the foo-niverse.

I heard *foobar* for the first time when I was auditing CSCI S-7 at Harvard Summer School taught by Henry Leitner. In a class where he was introducing the concept of an algorithm, he handed a student in the front row a dictionary and asked him to search for *foobar*. Of course, he didn't find it, and it prompted a lesson on approaches to search. I wrote down *foobar??* in the corner of my notes, and didn't think about it much until I sat down to write this blog post.

But I've had an epiphany and I need to share with you what's been revealed to me. I want to make something absolutely, explicitly clear, without any hint of ambig-foo-ity. **I love foobar**. It's a goofy, sweet word. It could be a fat golden retriever's name (with potential nicknames Fooby or Boofy). It could be a muppet. It could be a beloved family nickname, perhaps given by a toddler-age sibling who couldn't pronounce something like Robert or Theodore quite correctly. *"Oh, my folks call me Foobar. It's a long story."*

What I have gathered for you here today is the ultimate celebration of foobar and its meronym, foo. I have chosen to foo-cus on (rough) homophones rather than a strict adherence to spelling or direct relation to CS. Consequently, I have excluded *foo* [ˈɸɔː] ("in, inside") in Murui Huitoto, *phở* [fəː˧˩] in Vietnamese, and English words like *fool*, *foosball*, or *futon*. Because Fu/Foo is a very common last name, I have only included a few famous Fu/Foo/Fous, especially those whose work I admire.

So here's some foo-d for thought, and foo-sic for the soul. For the optimal experience, listen to [*Canon in Foo*](#foo-foo-song) while you browse.

<p>All of the other nonsense placeholder conventions (“<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metasyntactic_variable">metasyntactic variables</a>”) don’t seem to have the same <em>je ne sais foo</em>, if you know what I mean. Here’s a short list of examples: baz, qux, xyzzy, corge, grault, garply, waldo, fred, plugh, thud, dummy. To my ear, these all sound kind of dull or ungraceful, while <em>foobar</em> seems to roll off the tongue.</p>

<p><em>Foobar</em> has a tantalizing mystery about it. Nobody knows exactly where it began. The earliest foo appeared in the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smokey_Stover">Smokey Stover</a> comics in the 1930s. Then WWII gave us <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_military_slang_terms#FUBAR">FUBAR</a>, which I won’t spell out for you here. And from 1959 on, it entered computing and hacker culture, the apotheotic example coming from the <a href="https://www.gricer.com/tmrc/dictionary1959.html">Tech Model Railroad Club’s dictionary</a> at MIT, which calls FOO “the sacred syllable” and declares, “Our first obligation is to keep the Foo Counters turning.” For a more detailed etymology of <em>foobar</em>, see <a href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3092">RFC 3092</a>, published on April 1st, 2001 (foo-thousand-one).</p>

<p>The real reason we can’t seem to identify the true ur-foo is that foo is ubiquitous across languages. The consonant /f/ occurs in about 44% of languages, and /u/ in about 88% (<a href="https://phoible.org/">PHOIBLE</a>). If a language happens to have both, it’s a relatively unremarkable syllable. English, Scots, French, Mandarin, and Spanish all have some variation of /fu/. In Mandarin, with respect to tone, fu can mean husband/man (夫 fū), woman/wife (妇 fù), skin (肤 fū), good fortune (福 fú), clothing (服 fú), mansion/government office (府 fǔ), rotten (腐 fǔ), rich (富 fù), to float (浮 fú), to serve (服 fú), to carry/to lose (负 fù), to return (复 fù).</p>

It was just the time that someone combined it with *bar* in English that we were really cooking with fire. I'll show you: let's talk about the foo-netics of foobar. I'm going to focus specifically on General American English, because that's the dialect I'm most familiar with.

*Foobar* is pronounced [ˈfuˌbɑɹ], with primary stress on the first syllable and a secondary stress on the second. This is common in compound words like *blackbird*, *classmate*, or *hotdog*. Neither of the vowels reduce (to something like [ˈfubɚ], weird), and therefore it really does behave like the fuse of *foo* and *bar*. Both starting consonants /f/ and /b/ are labial, and the vowel sounds transition nicely from high back rounded [u] to low back unrounded [ɑ], almost like it was always meant to be said. *Foobar*...

Additionally, the graphetics of foobar are incredibly pleasant. It looks like it could be a naturally occurring word. Unlike my previous examples of *xyzzy* or *qux*, it has a shape shared by many common English words. It goes: consonant, vowel cluster, consonant, vowel, consonant. There's no weird clusters, no end-of-the-alphabet oddballs like x, y, or z. Could *phoobar* have been as famous? Could *fuhbahr*?

I think these linguistic properties contribute greatly to the memetic signi-foo-cance of foobar, especially in nerd world. It's become a kind of cultural touchstone, almost an easter egg. And it's kind of beautiful as well, because as a junk variable, foobar refers to nothing, but to us, foobar means everything.

Every time you define a variable as *foo*, know that you are participating in a decades-old practice with a rich linguistic and cultural history. If you are new to Computer Science, I hope that this knowledge guides you on your journey. If you are an old-timer, take a moment to tell your juniors a bit about the history of foobar. It's not a word in the [Merriam-Webster dictionary](https://www.harvard.com/book/9780877796688) (the exact book Henry Leitner tore in half to explain binary search), but it's written 4 million times on GitHub, in the archives of Usenet, and across all of our hearts.
