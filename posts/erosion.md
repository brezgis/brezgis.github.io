In undergrad I took two geology classes for my science requirement, because according to Reddit, they were the easiest possible sciences for my major. Sometimes I regret this, and I wish I had taken chemistry or physics or biology. There's things I am interested in now that I don't understand completely, and I know I'll probably never understand them at the depth I want. There's only so many hours in the day, and I do make choices.

But geology gave me one enduring gift, which is a small intuition for the confounds of scale. Geologists think in millions of years, the textbook said one time. The summer after I took this class, I went hiking in Acadia National Park. In many places, under your feet, you could see the little scrape marks of the ice from long ago, almost like the rock was washing away under you.

It makes me think that if humanity lived on a different timescale, mountains would rise and fall like waves and glaciers would lap at our feet. We only consider these things static because of our temporal relationship to their change. I also think of the long-running [pitch drop experiment](https://smp.uq.edu.au/pitch-drop-experiment). It has only dropped nine times in the last century. If the tar dropped like water, one moment ago García Márquez would have just died. 1938, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1970, 1979, 1988, 2000, and 2014, kap, kap, kap.

I feel it with my friends in graduate school. In one year, everything will be different, and we will all go live in different places. And I wonder about all of the things that are so precious and important now, they will exist only within us. We call the lounge with no windows "the Hole" like the way they call solitary confinement in the movies. The other day, it was the three of us reviewing linear algebra problems on one side of the table, the room dark and small and warm. The whole world in one room, and systems of equations staring back at us from the other side of it.

I get tastes of it now and again when my husband travels for work. But this almost seems the opposite of change. Our apartment, our great shared landscape, churning with the movement of everyday life: the sound of running water, the scream of the moka pot, the clacking of keys. Knowledge that all at rest will be undone and put back again in short order: the dishes, the laundry, the bedsheets. When I am alone, nothing moves unless I move it. I have the most direct causal power over my landscape.

And I know that one day, he will get a job in California, and I will again follow him across the country. And in my wake I will have to leave behind this wonderful life I have built here in Massachusetts, all my knowing of its strange crevices and rhythms. My coffeeshop, the trains, the stairs at Porter I walk up and down under the guise of maintaining my heart health within a sedentary lifestyle. I come up to the landing and then the street level panting and victorious. One day I had done this and gone straight to the eyebrow threading place. And I laid still and let the tech do her magic, the little stinging pull of the thread, purifying me, putting me back to order. She said, “Are you okay?” about my breathing, and I told her about the stairs, and we laughed. She asked me, “Why would you do that?”

I felt something similar when I moved out of Atlanta. I was finishing up my final course paper of undergrad, and we had decided to walk to the Trader Joe’s to get supplies for dinner. And I realized during the walk that it was the last time we would pass through those streets. They called it the garden district, but nobody actually did—the city called it that when they wanted to advertise events. Maks and I called it “the houses”, as in “do you want to go through the houses?” I would walk through by myself sometimes, to cut through to Piedmont Park. And while I walked, I looked at the different houses and decided which ones we would live in if we had the 2 million dollars to buy such a property. So when it was the last time, and we were checking out at the Trader Joe’s, I couldn’t help but feel the weight of the moment. We would never go there again. And so far, we haven’t, and probably never will.

Our home, it is in Alewife, which is so ugly and so beautiful, not exactly at the same time, but conditionally, like a piecewise function. It depends on which streets you take, or to what degree you choose to ignore the rate at which the gentrification apartment blocks are slowly eating the blighted industrial blocks. If you immerse yourself in the green part, it is breathtakingly beautiful. There are lots of animals. It was the very thing that compelled me to get off the bus from the lab one stop earlier, so I could cut through the nature preserve and see, at different times, ducks, young deer, a turtle, a muskrat. Or bike down to the reservoir and choose to look beyond the chain-link fence to the reflective and expansive blue.

The cracked sidewalk, the snow that turns to slush, the often late commuter rail that rolls up with indignant dinging: I am here! I am here! And the asphalt and the concrete and the bricks all mixed together like variety cereal on the street. I know it all so well, and soon it may cease to be mine.

The quiet of my therapist’s office, which is never actually so quiet in practice. You can hear the bell of the Central Square church on the hour, and that means that he has given me five more minutes than usual, because a conversation has run long or I’ve been difficult enough to need hundreds of seconds of staring to ease out a response from me. People’s voices rise up from the street, the cars, the radiator. A lot of it comes to me usually when I am looking at the rug. And I can see the scuffed toes of his oxfords, over the rug which is exactly the color of creamy tomato soup.

Or school, or any of the other schools. I have learned the shape of the lecture halls and seminar rooms by slipping into public colloquia. You can get anywhere without anyone saying a word by wearing a backpack. The comfort of fold-down seats in an auditorium with a slanted floor. One of those desks like from high school, you have to slip into the side, and you’re slotted into this mecha-structure which surrounds you and extends you into a human-seat hybrid, environmentally optimized for notetaking and hand raising. Holding you in its sad, silent, institutional lap. I always take notes on graph paper during the talks. I write hard and dark with a mechanical pencil, so often in the quiet of the talk, immediately surrounding me, are the sounds of my own doing: scratch, scratch, snap, click, scratch, scratch.

The STEM complex on campus which must have been sleek and modern in its time, now showing signs of its age. People leave up flyers for months, even years. Walking past an office with a flyer for an invited talk in spring of 2019. Did they see it too? Did they also not have the heart to take it down? Because they know as well that all changes and we are left ultimately with detritus? Change and its products and sheddings: crispy insect shells and thick dust.

My friends and I walk through the hallway and we are the only loud things in it, our voices ricocheting through the hall. One day, one of the weary PhD students came out of their lounge and she told me, "I always know when it's you coming. I don't know what you're saying, but I can hear your voice." And therefore it's all ours, stealing pizza from the STEM events, "let's discuss our favorite atomic element" as a silly fake social disguise, dancing around on the tile under the fluorescent lights. So we are standing there talking about boron or helium, grabbing a plate full of buffalo chicken pizza and a ginger ale for good measure.

Or the people I know from my husband’s work, with big lopsided easygoing grins. They make the robots do tricks all day, clicking their weights into place, a voluminous stream of yes, no, yes, yes. They stream Spot’s brain into Spot’s yellow, jerking body, and the movements he makes are strikingly animalistic, almost aggressive, almost like the clumsy young deer stumbling out from the brush. They aren’t quite comfortable yet with their long legs.

Or perhaps Spot moves like an old arthritic dog. How we are only smooth and continuous at some great but bounded interval in our lives. And at the beginning and the end we are quite the same: jerky, unstable, uncalibrated, in desperate need of help but fervent desire of independence. One push to the mechanism that supplies our power, and we too, fall to the mat lifeless.

Sometimes I watch Spot back himself into the charger, where there’s a big, chunky QR code that calls him home. Does he see it and feel the way I feel when Alewife station’s ugly pink brick appears through the windows of the train? Does it feel like that warm, grateful rush I got when I was a kid in the backseat of my parents’ minivan, rounding the corner back to our neighborhood after a vacation? Or is it tired? Is it like a little death? There Spot sits until somebody decides to take him out and play with him again, to let him complete tasks, to let him grab soft objects with his hand-mouth.

Like they named Laika before they sent her to space, the researchers name them all human names: Fausto and Quinn and Lenny and Betty. They buy them actual dog toys, little plush ones with squeakers. And sometimes their legs break, and the plastic goes everywhere, and they have to send them down a floor so the hardware folks can get them in working order again. We have a Spot-Leg in our apartment, this big yellow banana thing that's hanging off of a peg in my husband's office. It's shaped like the triangle hinge leg on a grasshopper. A trophy, a memory.

All of his coworkers are within a few years of me, and yet I feel like there is an uncrossable chasm of difference between us. Like there is something so comfortable and casual within them, and I haven’t and never will learn it entirely. Whatever has been done to my weights in time was never done correctly or never fully implemented. And I think what is going on is a silent stream of no, no, no. No path found to target state, negative reward. Log-probability of social ease: negative infinity. And yet they seem to like me relentlessly and without effort. I wonder if their liking of my husband just extends to me naturally. Like there is Maks and there is extra-Maks-mini, who is strange and uncomfortable but nevertheless, a part of Maks which must be accommodated.

Probably like my mother-in-law, who likes me effortlessly. Anna is here! Hello Anna, how is your mood! Anna, you got skinny! On the phone call, her sweet and rounded voice. I always liked the way Russian phonetics sound. Like buttery popcorn in your mouth, like a blue Jolly Rancher clicking against your teeth. Zsh, shh, tsh, soft luh. She likes me because I am of her family now, and there’s nothing either of us can do about it. I like that too. It’s settled. I’m no awkward girlfriend or family friend. They have to like me, because I will raise their grandchildren.

Or maybe because she never got a daughter, since she had three boys. She braids my hair clumsily, and I let her. She doesn’t think to smooth the little pieces that get caught around my ears. I think about being a little kid, and whining and screaming at my mom, because I could feel the seam of my sock or the tag on my shirt or my ponytail was tugging in some small specific way. I’m too polite to say anything so I leave the lumpy braid in until it falls out on its own.
